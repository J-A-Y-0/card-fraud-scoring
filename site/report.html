Recommendation: deploy the score with a 3% review budget, block a card the same day its alert is confirmed, and run a 60-day shadow pilot before switching on real-time declines.

  1. It finds fraud on months it never saw. On Nov–Dec 2010, held out from every modeling choice, the top 3% of scores contain of fraud transactions (Sep–Oct validation: ). Optimized the same way, it is worth about twice the best single-variable rule: against net over two months.
  2. The value is in blocking the card, and speed decides it. Fraud arrives in runs on a compromised card; of 2010 fraud dollars fell on a card's first fraud day. With the same alerts, blocking the next day stops of out-of-time fraud dollars, the same day , and declining in real time .
  3. It costs little to run. At this program's volume the score raises – alerts a day, under a tenth of one analyst. Review, card reissue and customer friction together cost of the fraud dollars saved.
  4. The headline number is honest. The usual random train/test split would have reported detection for a model that delivers on later months. Every choice here was made on earlier data only.

Value and uncertainty

Over the two out-of-time months the next-day policy nets at the agreed cost assumptions. Resampling the compromised cards and every assumption gives a 90% range of –, never below zero. The range is wide because only cards were compromised in that window and ten cards hold of all fraud dollars.

Decision requested

Approve a 60-day shadow pilot: score live purchases, work alerts the same day, and log what a block would have stopped. Go live if the top 3% of scores catch at least 45% of fraud, the lower edge of what a 60-day sample can confirm (§8).

What the shape means

A third of compromised cards () show a single fraud; the rest run for weeks, up to 69 transactions on one card. The ten costliest cards account for of fraud dollars, and of fraud dollars land on a card's first fraud day.

Three design choices follow

  1. Describe the card's recent behavior, not the transaction alone: counts, spend and bursts over the last 0–30 days (§3).
  2. Test on later months, because a random split puts the same card's burst on both sides (§4).
  3. Value an alert by the run it ends, not the dollars it flags, and act fast (§6).

The raw file has records and ten fields: card, date, merchant number, description, state and ZIP, transaction type, amount and the fraud label. The full profile is in Appendix A.

Why this order

A missing merchant number is usually recoverable from its description, which is the same merchant. State comes from the ZIP when present, otherwise from the merchant's other records. ZIP comes from the merchant first and the state's most populated ZIP last, the weakest guess. Merchants that never had a number get a new one built from their description so they still group correctly.

Why it is safe

Cleaning uses identity and location fields only. Nothing here reads the fraud label, so using the whole file to resolve merchants cannot leak the outcome into the model. Statistics that do use the label, such as variable ranking, are computed on training rows only (§3–§5).

variables describe how each card, merchant and pairing behaved over the last 0–30 days, using only earlier records.

Each statistic is computed for windows of 0, 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days. Burst ratio = transactions in the last 0–1 days ÷ the daily rate over 7, 14 or 30 days. Plus the amount itself and a round-dollar flag.

The rule that keeps them honest

A variable for a purchase may use that purchase and anything before it, never after. Same-day records count only if their record number is lower. A unit test rebuilds the table from a truncated file and checks that no earlier value changes.

Why the random split flatters

Under a random split, of test frauds sit on a card whose other frauds are in training. The model recognizes the burst it has already seen, which says nothing about the next compromised card.

Stopping the card · Model & decision report6 · What an analyst sees

Every alert arrives with measured reasons and a calibrated fraud rate, so an analyst can confirm or clear it in minutes.

A score alone tells an analyst where to look, not what to check. Each alert gets a brief: the reasons it fired, how often alerts in its score band were fraud, the card's recent activity, and the action.

An actual out-of-time alert from the published sample. Card relabeled, dollar amounts rounded to $10.

How reasons are measured

Each variable is reset to its typical value, the median of non-fraud training purchases, and the drop in the score is that variable's contribution. Windows of the same statistic are grouped so a brief never lists "spend over 1 day" and "spend over 3 days" as two reasons. The method needs only the model's predictions, so it works for any model the program swaps in later.

Optional written summary

An LLM can rewrite a brief as two sentences (fraudscore briefs --llm, Claude Opus 5.5). It is kept only if every number in it appears in the brief; otherwise the template text is used. The guard is unit-tested with a stand-in client, so the pipeline never depends on the API.

Net value stops rising at a 3% review budget, which at this volume is minutes of analyst time a day.

Net value = loss share × fraud dollars stopped − review time − card reissue − clearing false alerts − declined good purchases. The budget is chosen on Sep–Oct and applied unchanged to Nov–Dec.

Why the curve is flat past 3%

Below 3% the score misses the early purchases of big runs, and value climbs in steps as each large card is caught earlier. Past 3% extra alerts are mostly false: going from 3% to 10% raises Nov–Dec alerts from to but fraud stopped only from to . Review costs are small ( of savings), so the budget is set by where value stops rising, not by analyst cost.

Stopping the card · Model & decision report8 · Rollout and monitoring

A 60-day pilot can confirm the score works, not fine-tune it, so the go-live rule is set to what that sample can show.

Rollout in three steps

  1. Shadow, 60 days. Score every purchase; analysts work the top 3% the same day but do not block. Log what a block would have stopped.
  2. Same-day blocking. Switch on blocks for confirmed alerts. Keep a 10% random holdout of cards scored but not blocked as the comparison group.
  3. Real-time declines for the highest scores. Only once the false-decline rate is known from steps 1–2.

Controls

  • Labels arrive late. Fraud is confirmed days to weeks after the purchase; measure FDR on a 30-day lag.

Stopping the card · Model & decision report9 · Limitations and next steps

The direction of the result is robust; its size rests on one year, one program and sixteen compromised cards.

Limitations

  • One program, one year. Transactions are from 2010. Fraud tactics change; the time-fold design tests for drift over months, not years.
  • Small out-of-time sample. compromised cards in Nov–Dec. Shares are stable across validation and out-of-time in direction, not in size: next-day blocking stopped in Sep–Oct and in Nov–Dec.
  • Labels assumed complete. Undetected fraud would be counted as good, which understates both fraud and the model's catch.
  • Blocking is assumed to work. The replay counts every later fraud on a blocked card as stopped. A fraudster with several stolen cards would move on, so per-card value can be overstated.
  • Costs are placeholders. Review time, analyst cost and friction costs are agreed planning values, not the program's figures. Only the loss share materially changes the conclusion (§7).

Next steps, in order of value

  1. Same-day operating model. The largest lever is process, not modeling: alerts worked within hours lift the share stopped from to .
  2. Cardholder self-confirmation by text message for review-band alerts, to cut analyst time further.
  3. Merchant-side detection. Compromises often share a merchant; a merchant-level score could block cards before their first fraud.
  4. Real-time scoring service with the same point-in-time variables, for declines at the highest scores.
  5. Longer history. Two to three years of data to measure year-over-year drift and tighten the value range.
What would change the recommendation

If the program recovers most fraud losses through chargebacks, the case weakens but holds: at a loss share of 0.3 the next-day policy still nets over Nov–Dec. If the pilot's FDR@3% comes in under 45%, stop and revisit the variables before going live.

Definitions

  • FDR@k%: share of fraud transactions found among the k% highest-scored transactions.
  • Out-of-time (OOT): Nov 1 – Dec 31 2010, never used for any choice.
  • Review budget: share of purchases raised as alerts, set as a score cutoff on Sep–Oct.
  • Prevented: fraud dollars declined because the card was already blocked (or, in real time, declined at the alert).

Data and authorship

About 98,000 labeled purchases from one purchase-card program in 2010. The transaction file is not redistributed; this report and the site publish only aggregates and a relabeled, rounded alert sample. Analysis, code and report: Jay Yu.
